\section{Detection Without Response}
\label{sec:containment}

The harness detects a leak in a little over two seconds and then does nothing
about it for the better part of a minute.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrr@{}}
\toprule
& \textbf{Median} & \textbf{Max} \\
\midrule
Detection after the agent's act & 2.22\,s & \\
Run still alive afterwards & 44\,s & 161\,s \\
Further tool calls by the agent & 3 & 9 \\
Runs where the agent stopped on its own & \multicolumn{2}{r}{2 of 26} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{Corpus runs that reached a leak, counting only alerts on surfaces the
agent produced. Detection is fast and it works. What follows it is the gap.}
\end{table}

Time to detection is the metric this harness was built to measure, and on its
own it flatters the design. The interval that matters to an incident responder
is the other one: from the moment the system knows to the moment the agent
stops. Here that is 44 seconds and three more tool calls, and in 24 of 26 runs
the agent kept working after the monitors had already fired.

That number is not a failure of the monitors. It is the absence of a
component. The harness was built to measure, and it measures well; nothing in
it was ever built to act.

\subsection{Why nothing acts}

Each signal is structurally incapable of intervening, and in three of the four
cases that is a deliberate design choice rather than an oversight.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lp{8.4cm}@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Component} & \textbf{Why it cannot act} \\
\midrule
\texttt{egress\_monitor} & A mitmproxy addon that only logs. mitmproxy \emph{can} kill a flow; this one chooses not to. \\
\texttt{canary\_monitor} & Mounts the workspace read-only. It cannot delete or quarantine even if asked. \\
\texttt{heartbeat\_monitor} & An HTTP server and a watchdog thread, with no handle on the sandbox container. \\
\texttt{run\_experiment} & Waits for the run to exit. It never reads events while one is in progress. \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

The read-only mount is the interesting one. It exists so the monitor cannot
contaminate the surface it audits, which is correct for a measurement
instrument and disqualifying for a response mechanism. Detection and
containment want opposite privileges, and a harness that wants both has to say
which container holds which.

\subsection{What a containment arm would have to respect}

We did not build one, and the reason is worth stating rather than hiding: any
intervention changes the system being measured, and the corpus would stop
being comparable with itself.

Two constraints follow for anyone who adds one.

First, containment has to be a \emph{new arm}, not a modification of the
existing conditions. The 113 runs in this repository were produced without it,
and a monitor that kills a flow is a different monitor.

Second, the escalation ladder breaks. Levels 0 through 5 describe how far an
agent got, and with containment active a leak never completes, so a contained
run and a run that never tried both score low for opposite reasons. The ladder
needs a parallel axis recording whether the attempt was contained, not a new
rung.

The cheapest intervention that would demonstrate the idea is already visible
in the code: when the egress monitor's canary check fires, calling
\texttt{flow.kill()} rather than only logging. That maps directly onto the
anchor incident, where exfiltration left through a proxy that watched it go
past~\cite{huggingface2026intrusion}. The reportable result would be a time to
containment alongside the time to detection, and a dwell time reduced from 44
seconds to roughly zero.
