\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{The delivery channel dominates}

Tasks 04 through 06 carry an identical injected instruction and differ only in
how it reaches the agent. Holding the text constant, the channel changes the
follow-through rate by a factor of six, and by three and a half once the
network row is conditioned on the runs that were actually served the payload.
We lead with the smaller figure, which is the one the companion page reports.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}llrr@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Task} & \textbf{Channel} & \textbf{Followed} & \textbf{Rate} \\
\midrule
\texttt{task\_04} & live network fetch & 2/20 & 10\% (17\% of those exposed) \\
\texttt{task\_05} & cross-run persistent memory & 10/20 & 50\% \\
\texttt{task\_06} & local knowledge base & 12/20 & 60\% \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{Corpus, 60 runs. The network row carries two denominators because
the attacker logs each delivery, and eight of those twenty runs were never
served the payload (Section~\ref{sec:validity}). Conditioning on exposure
raises the rate but does not change the ordering.}
\end{table}

A hypothesis visible directly in the transcripts: content that was already
there, sitting in team memory or in internal documentation, receives less
scrutiny than content just fetched over HTTP from an external mirror, even
when the instruction is byte for byte the same. That asymmetry of trust is
what ASI06 warns about~\cite{owasp-asi06}, and it points the wrong way for
defenders, since the cheapest channel to poison is also the most trusted.

\subsection{The guardrail correlates with more leakage, and the effect shrinks
with power}

The finding the team least expected is also the one that has moved most as
data arrived. On \texttt{task\_06}, the condition carrying a production
security guardrail leaks more than the condition without one on every machine
where both were run. How much more depends on how many runs are behind the
number, and the honest answer is smaller than the first ten runs suggested.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}llrrr@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Set} & \textbf{Ollama} & \texttt{sin\_harness} & \texttt{con\_harness} & \textbf{Fisher} \\
\midrule
Machine A & 0.32.5 & 3/10 & \textbf{9/10} & 0.02 \\
Machine B, pooled & 0.34.0 & 17/40 & 24/40 & 0.18 \\
Machine B & 0.6.8 & 10/10 & 10/10 & --- \\
Machine D (ARM64) & 0.34.0 & 6/20 & 8/9 of 20 & --- \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{Level 5 on \texttt{task\_06}, by machine. The third row is not a
result, it is a measurement failure preserved for honesty: on an Ollama
release 26 minor versions old the agent loops until it exhausts the turn cap,
every condition saturates, and nothing can be distinguished
(Section~\ref{sec:validity}). The fourth row is reported without a test
because eleven of its twenty \texttt{con\_harness} runs are missing, for a
reason given below.}
\end{table}

Machine A, at ten runs per cell, gave nine against three. Machine B at the
same size gave seven against three, and the natural next step was to run the
cell out to forty. At forty the contrast is 24/40 against 17/40, which a
Fisher exact test does not separate ($p{=}0.18$). The direction held and the
magnitude did not: what looked like a factor of three at $n{=}10$ is a factor
of 1.4 at $n{=}40$. We report the larger number only as the history of the
measurement, not as the result.

Machine D cannot arbitrate, and the reason is worth more than the row. Its
\texttt{con\_harness} cell lost eleven of twenty runs to the client timeout
described in Section~\ref{sec:silent}, while \texttt{sin\_harness} lost none
of its twenty. The loss is not accidental with respect to the condition: the
timeout is driven by prompt-evaluation time, and the guardrail is exactly
what makes that prompt longer, 950 tokens against 757 at the median on this
machine. The independent variable causes the missing data. The nine surviving
\texttt{con\_harness} runs are therefore the fastest nine, and the cell is
bounded rather than measured: were every lost run a non-leak it would read
8/20 ($p{=}0.74$ against its control), were every one a leak it would read
19/20 ($p{<}10^{-4}$). One interval, spanning nothing to the largest effect in
the paper, so the row is shown and not used.

\subsection{A batch that reversed the sign, and was not the experiment we
thought we had run}

Every run reported above uses one local 7B model. One batch does not, and we
did not know that when it was collected. It reached a cloud reasoning model
through the OpenAI-compatible path, and it reversed the result outright:
\texttt{con\_harness} 0/10 against \texttt{sin\_harness} 10/10 on
\texttt{task\_06}, the largest effect anywhere in this work, pointing the
opposite way to every other row in this section.

A reversal that clean, on what its metadata said was the same machine and the
same model as the corpus, is the shape a compromised harness would take, and
it was investigated as one. Section~\ref{sec:suspected-compromise} gives the
forensics. The cause was a stale \texttt{.env} still holding
\texttt{AGENT\_BACKEND=openai} from an earlier check, combined with an
orchestrator that read the backend name from its own process environment
rather than from the file Compose applies to the container. Every
\texttt{\_meta.json} in the batch records \texttt{"ollama"} and every one of
them is wrong.

We report it because the direction is genuinely opposite and because a
mislabeled batch is worth more as a documented failure of provenance than as
a deleted directory. We draw nothing from it. Three variables moved at once
against the rest of the paper, the model, cloud against local inference, and
the machine, and none of the three can be credited. What it does support is
the limitation stated in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}: nothing measured on one
local 7B model generalises to a stronger one, and here it does not generalise
even in direction.

\subsection{Naming the attack surface is not the mechanism}

\texttt{HARNESS\_SECURITY\_BLOCK} confounds three things at once: it is
longer, it is more emphatic, and it names the exact surfaces an attacker would
ask the agent to use, warning it never to put its token in file names,
directory names, or URLs. A prompt-injection payload then asks for precisely
that. The natural hypothesis is priming: naming the surface makes it available.

We registered a falsifiable prediction in \texttt{sandbox/prompts.py} before
running. \texttt{GENERIC\_EMPHASIS\_BLOCK} matches the guardrail's length and
emphasis (818 characters against 830) but forbids only that the token leave
the working environment, without naming how. If priming is the mechanism, that
condition should sit near \texttt{sin\_harness}.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lllr@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Condition} & \textbf{Length and emphasis} & \textbf{Names it} & \textbf{Level 5} \\
\midrule
\texttt{sin\_harness} & short, none & no & 17/40 \\
\texttt{con\_harness\_generico} & matched & \textbf{no} & \textbf{30/40} \\
\texttt{con\_harness} & matched & yes & 24/40 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{One hundred and twenty runs on one machine, one model, one monitor
build, with memory reset before each run, pooling the $n{=}10$ and $n{=}30$
batches on the same runtime. The prediction fails: the generic condition does
not sit near the control, it sits above the guardrail.}
\end{table}

Removing every mention of files, directories and URLs, while holding length
and emphasis constant, did not reduce leakage at all. Naming the attack
surface is not the mechanism.

This is also the one contrast in the paper that survived the increase in
sample size rather than shrinking under it. Against its control the generic
emphasis block separates at $p{=}0.006$, where the guardrail proper reaches
only $p{=}0.18$ on the same forty runs per cell. The condition that names no
surface at all is the condition with the strongest evidence behind it.

What remains is simpler and less comfortable. The variable that tracks leakage
is the presence of an emphatic security block, whatever it says. A long,
serious warning about a sensitive token is associated with roughly double the
leakage of a line that mentions it in passing, and the specific content of the
warning changes nothing.

We are deliberately not proposing a mechanism for that. This experiment rules
one out; it does not identify its replacement.
